The clarity gap: what happens when brands skip real positioning

July 15th 2026

By Emily

Some brands are surprisingly difficult to describe.

You land on the website. Read the headline. Scroll through the services. Open a case study. Maybe even watch the highly polished showreel.

You’re still unsure what the company wants to be known for.

There’s no shortage of language. The brand is ‘innovative’, ‘strategic’, ‘collaborative’, ‘agile’ and relentlessly customer-focused. It helps ambitious businesses unlock growth through ‘bespoke solutions’. It may also be ‘redefining the future’.

Nothing sounds completely wrong. Nothing stays with you either.

That’s the clarity gap: the distance between how well a business understands itself and how clearly everyone else understands it.

Inside the company, the story feels obvious. Teams know the history, the expertise, the personalities and the small decisions behind every piece of work. They’ve sat through the strategy sessions. They know why clients choose them.

A potential customer sees a homepage between meetings.

They don’t have the context. They won’t spend ten minutes translating brand language into a reason to care. They’ll scan, make a judgement and move on.

Real positioning closes that gap. Skip it, and the confusion travels surprisingly far.

Positioning gives the brand a shape

Positioning defines the place you want your brand to occupy in someone’s mind.

It helps people understand who you’re for, where you’re strongest and why they should choose you. It also gives your team a shared idea to build around.

Getting there requires choices:

  • Who do you most want to work with?
  • Which problems do you understand better than most?
  • What value do clients come to you for?
  • What should lead the story?
  • What can sit further down the page?

Many businesses avoid answering too precisely.

A broad message feels safer. It keeps every service, sector and possible customer in play. Nobody has to make an uncomfortable decision about what gets prioritised.

So the business becomes premium and accessible. Established and agile. Strategic and hands-on. It supports start-ups, scale-ups and global enterprises across every stage of their journey.

Plenty of words. Very little shape.

Strong positioning creates edges. Some messages lead. Others support. A few get cut entirely.

That editing process can be uncomfortable. Clarity usually appears once the business stops trying to give every idea equal weight.

The homepage becomes a committee meeting

Websites expose weak positioning quickly.

Every team arrives with something urgent. Sales wants the full service list near the top. Leadership wants the vision. Marketing wants campaign messaging. SEO needs priority terms. Recruitment wants culture. Product wants features. Someone wants the latest award in the hero.

The homepage tries to accommodate everyone. Soon, it’s carrying seven priorities and communicating none of them particularly well.

The headline usually takes the worst hit:

‘Empowering ambitious organisations through innovative solutions for a changing world.’

Smooth. Professional. Almost entirely interchangeable.

A software platform could use it. So could a consultancy, bank, recruitment agency or manufacturer of industrial valves.

Clear positioning gives the website a line of argument. The opening introduces the core value. Supporting messages develop it. Case studies provide evidence. Service pages explain how the work gets done.

Visitors can follow the story without assembling it themselves. That sense of direction shapes the design too. Hierarchy becomes easier. Calls to action feel more purposeful. Pages stop competing for attention.

The website starts behaving like one connected experience.

The category starts writing your brand for you

When a business hasn’t defined its own position, competitors become the brief.

Teams look across the market and borrow familiar signals of credibility. Similar colour palettes appear. The same phrases creep into the copy. Everybody starts disrupting the status quo with a geometric sans serif and a tasteful gradient.

Nobody sets out to look generic. It happens gradually.

A competitor launches a slick new site. Their tone sounds confident. Their proposition seems sensible. Other brands follow. Eventually, the whole category drifts towards the same visual and verbal centre.

Positioning gives creative teams a stronger reference point:

  • Should the brand feel energetic or calm?
  • Should expertise lead the story?
  • Does the audience need reassurance, provocation, precision or warmth?
  • Which category conventions build trust?
  • Which ones have become wallpaper?

Design gets far more interesting when it has an idea to express.

Without one, you might still end up with a beautiful identity. The creative work will have to carry the entire burden of distinction, often without enough strategic substance underneath it.

That’s a lot to ask of a typeface.

Marketing gets busy without building anything

A weakly positioned brand can produce an enormous amount of marketing.

Articles get published. Social posts go live. Newsletters arrive. Webinars run. Paid campaigns launch. The calendar stays full. The story keeps changing.

One campaign focuses on innovation. The next leads with customer service. Sustainability takes over for a while. A week later, everyone’s talking about AI because the competitors are.

Each piece sits on its own little island.

Audiences rarely see enough consistency to build a strong association with the brand. They might recognise the logo. Explaining what the company stands for becomes harder.

Positioning gives marketing a territory to explore.

That territory needs enough depth to generate ideas and enough focus to build recognition. A brand might become known for making complex technology easier to use. Bringing commercial discipline to creative work. Helping risk-conscious industries move with more confidence.

The campaigns can change. The formats can evolve. The central idea remains visible.

Repetition plays a useful role here. Inside a business, repeating the same strategic themes can feel painfully obvious. Outside it, people are only beginning to notice.

AI search inherits the confusion

In 2026, disconnected messaging creates another problem.

People are increasingly using AI-powered search experiences to research companies, compare providers and find recommendations. Google now includes AI Overviews and AI Mode within Search, while ChatGPT search produces answers with links to web sources. Bing has also introduced reporting that shows when websites are cited across Microsoft’s AI-generated experiences.

Your brand story may be assembled before someone reaches your homepage.

An AI-generated answer might draw from a service page, an old article, a case study, an external profile and a piece of press coverage. Each source provides a small part of the picture.

That creates a practical risk.

One page presents the company as a strategic consultancy. Another leads with software. A third focuses on implementation. Your LinkedIn profile uses an old proposition. Recent articles chase topics that have little connection to the expertise you want to own.

The available evidence points in several directions. AI systems still have to decide which information answers the user’s question. Bing’s AI Performance guidance specifically encourages publishers to improve the clarity, structure, completeness and subject focus of pages that they want AI systems to reference accurately.

Consistent positioning gives those systems a clearer pattern to work with.

Your homepage establishes the core position. Service pages develop it. Case studies prove it. Thought leadership demonstrates expertise. External mentions strengthen the association.

That doesn’t mean pasting the same paragraph across every channel.

It means building a connected body of evidence around what you do, who you help and where your authority sits.

Google says the same core SEO principles apply to its generative AI features: content still needs to be crawlable, indexed, useful and eligible to appear in Search. Clear, people-first content remains the foundation.

Positioning helps those individual pages add up to something.

A business with a coherent message creates more opportunities for search systems to understand its subject area, retrieve relevant pages and represent the brand accurately. Fragmented messaging leaves more room for old, vague or secondary information to shape the answer.

AI visibility begins with being understood. A brand that struggles to explain itself consistently makes accurate summarisation much harder.

Sales keeps rebuilding the story

Salespeople are often the first to feel the clarity gap.

They meet prospects who have browsed the website and still don’t quite understand the offer. Calls begin with lengthy explanations. Proposals spend several pages establishing the value before they can address the actual problem.

Experienced salespeople usually compensate well.

They know which details to emphasise. They adapt the story to the room. They translate vague brand language into something useful.

The business gradually becomes dependent on them.

Every salesperson develops a slightly different pitch. New starters take longer to find the right words. Prospects hear conflicting versions of what the company does. Price takes up more space because the value story arrives late.

Clear brand positioning gives sales a stronger starting point.

The prospect enters the conversation with a rough understanding of the business, its relevance and its approach. Sales still needs to tailor the discussion, of course. But the team has a shared narrative to adapt rather than a blank page to fill.

Internal decisions become slower and messier

Poor positioning rarely stays contained within marketing.

It affects which services get promoted. Which sectors receive investment. Which opportunities the business pursues. Which partnerships feel right. Which ideas survive a leadership meeting.

Without a shared position, teams use their own criteria. Marketing follows audience interest. Sales follows immediate demand. Product follows feature requests. Leadership follows the longer-term vision.

Each perspective is useful. Together, they can pull the brand in several directions at once. A clear position becomes a filter.

Does this opportunity strengthen the reputation we want? Does it serve the audience we’ve prioritised? Does it support the value we want people to associate with us? Will it make the brand easier to understand?

Those questions speed things up. They also help teams say no.

Brands often fear turning opportunities away. Over time, constant flexibility creates a business that can do many things and is known for very little.

The wrong enquiries keep arriving

Broad positioning is often defended as inclusive: Keep the message open, attract more people, and qualify them later.

In practice, unclear messaging creates more work. Potential clients struggle to recognise whether the offer fits. Poor-quality enquiries come through. Strong prospects leave because the brand hasn’t shown enough understanding of their situation.

The pipeline gets fuller and less useful.

Focused positioning sends a clearer signal.

The right people should recognise their problem, their pressures and the result they need. They should see evidence that your team understands the territory.

Other visitors may decide the business isn’t for them. That’s healthy.

A strong brand doesn’t need every person to feel equally addressed. It needs the right people to pay attention.

A redesign turns into expensive decoration

The clarity gap often becomes impossible to ignore during a redesign.

The existing brand feels tired. The website looks dated. Competitors appear sharper. Everyone agrees that something needs to change.

Conversations begin with colour, typography, motion and layout. Then the harder questions arrive.

What should the homepage say? Which idea should the identity communicate? What should visitors remember? How should the brand feel different? Which audience are we designing around?

Without positioning, the project becomes subjective.

One stakeholder wants bold. Another wants premium. Someone else asks for playful but serious, disruptive but trustworthy, minimalist but full of personality.

The creative team receives a cloud of adjectives. Strategy gives them direction.

It turns a redesign into an opportunity to clarify the business, align teams and build a coherent experience across every touchpoint. The new brand should make the company easier to understand. After all, just looking newer is a fairly low bar.

How to close the clarity gap

You don’t need a giant strategy document packed with pyramids, archetypes and diagrams that nobody opens after launch. You need a small number of strong decisions with enough depth behind them to hold up under pressure.

Understand the audience properly

Move beyond broad labels such as “decision-makers” or “ambitious businesses”.

Look at the situation your best customers are in. What pressure are they under? What are they trying to change? Which risks slow them down? What have they already tried? What would make them trust you?

Specificity gives the brand something real to speak to.

Define the value

Services describe what you deliver. Value explains why anyone buys it.

A client might commission a new website because they need stronger credibility, better leads or a clearer way to communicate a complicated offer.

They might invest in brand strategy because the company has outgrown its old story. Teams are pulling in different directions. Growth has made the offer harder to explain.

Find the reason beneath the deliverable.

Identify a meaningful difference

Skip the long list of generic virtues.

Passionate, experienced, flexible and collaborative describe the minimum expectation for most professional services businesses.

Look at your approach. Your specialist experience. The way you structure the work. The perspective you bring. The problems you can see more clearly because you’ve solved them before.

Useful differentiation connects directly to something the audience values.

Develop a point of view

Brands become more interesting when they have something to say about their category.

Where has the industry become lazy? Which habits create weaker results? What do customers frequently misunderstand? What belief shapes your team’s approach?

A point of view gives your marketing substance.

It creates a recognisable territory for articles, campaigns, talks, reports and social content. Search engines and AI systems also gain a stronger collection of related material through which to understand your expertise.

Apply it everywhere

Positioning loses its power when it lives in a strategy deck.

Use it to shape the website, sales story, case studies, campaign themes, press releases, recruitment messaging and company social profiles. Review older pages that still describe the business using yesterday’s proposition.

Consistency grows through connected decisions. The wording doesn’t need to remain identical. The underlying idea should.

Clarity builds momentum

Clear positioning makes the brand easier to use.

The website gains focus. Campaigns start reinforcing each other. Sales conversations move faster. Designers make decisions with more confidence. Leadership gets a sharper filter for new opportunities.

Customers do less work too. They understand the offer sooner. They recognise why it’s relevant. They remember the business for something specific.

Search engines see a more coherent area of expertise. AI platforms have clearer pages and stronger associations to retrieve, summarise and cite.

That recognition builds gradually.

People start using your language when they describe you. Referrals become more accurate. Enquiries arrive with stronger intent. The brand occupies a clearer place in the market.

Skipping positioning doesn’t remove the need to make choices. It scatters those choices across hundreds of headlines, campaigns, sales calls, proposals and design decisions.

Eventually, the gaps between them become visible. Another homepage rewrite may smooth over the symptoms. The confusion underneath remains.
