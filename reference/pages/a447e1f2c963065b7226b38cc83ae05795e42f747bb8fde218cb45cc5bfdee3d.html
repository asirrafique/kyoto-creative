6 brands we rate because they commit to the bit.

July 1st 2026

By Emily

Most brands can make a nice logo. Fewer can build a world people want to step into, talk about and spend money in.

A polished visual identity might win you a second look. But a clear, committed brand idea gives people a reason to remember you once they’ve scrolled on.

The brands below work because they don’t treat branding as the layer that sits on top of the business. Their point of view runs through the product, packaging, language, content, customer experience, and the decisions they make when nobody’s looking.

Ffern: Fragrance for people who hate being sold fragrance

Ffern makes perfume feel less like a trip to a department store and more like joining a small seasonal ritual.

The British fragrance brand releases limited-edition scents around the solstices and equinoxes, with each bottle accompanied by artwork, samples and carefully considered details from its wider world. You don’t simply browse a wall of fragrances and pick the least intimidating one. You join the ledger, wait for the next release and decide whether this particular season is for you.

Everything about it feels slow, thoughtful and slightly ceremonial. The language is poetic without being too precious. The packaging feels like something worth keeping. Even the limited availability feels tied to the way the product is made, rather than a big flashing sign screaming “HURRY UP”.

What we rate:

They’ve made scarcity part of the experience, not a sales tactic.

Ffern understands that a perfume is already an emotional purchase. Instead of shouting louder than every other fragrance brand, it creates anticipation, intimacy and a reason to pay attention.

Fishwife: Tinned fish with main-character energy

Tinned fish has spent most of its life hiding at the back of kitchen cupboards, somewhere between the baked beans and that packet of pasta you bought in 2021.

Fishwife looked at that category and saw an opportunity.

Its tins are colourful, playful and completely unashamed of themselves. The brand has built a personality around pantry food through bold packaging, punchy copy, recipes, merch and collaborations with brands such as Fly By Jing. It sells fish, yes. But it also sells the feeling that eating sardines from a tin can be a personality trait.

That takes more than good design. Fishwife has turned an overlooked product into a cultural object people want to put on their kitchen shelves, share on Instagram and give as presents.

What we rate:

They found a category with zero glamour and gave it a proper world.

The brand doesn’t pretend tinned fish is something it isn’t. It leans into the weirdness, the convenience and the joy of it, then gives people a reason to care.

Vacation: Sunscreen from an alternate reality

Vacation does not behave like a typical sunscreen brand.

Its world sits somewhere between a 1980s resort brochure, a corporate wellness programme and an infomercial you’d accidentally watch at 2am. The product names, campaign films, visual identity and writing all belong to the same gloriously odd universe.

Even the product experience carries the idea through. Vacation has built Poolsuite FM into its packaging through QR codes, turning sunscreen into an entry point to its retro leisure fantasy.

It would have been easy to stop at a nostalgic colour palette and a few palm trees. Instead, Vacation has kept expanding the joke, the world and the experience around it.

What we rate:

They didn’t stop at a visual style. They built a fictional universe around the product.

That level of commitment gives the brand range. A sunscreen can become fragrance. A product launch can become a piece of entertainment. A customer can become an honorary employee with a very silly job title.

The idea keeps generating new places to go.

Mother Root: A drinks brand that doesn’t need to pretend it is alcohol

Non-alcoholic drinks often borrow their cues from wine, spirits or beer. Dark bottles. Moody photography. A lot of trying very hard to prove they belong at the table.

Mother Root takes a different route.

Its aperitifs feel grown-up, botanical and a little mysterious, but never worthy or restrictive. The brand builds around its own flavour profile and ritual: apple cider vinegar, ginger, chilli, slow sipping and drinks that feel made for an actual evening rather than a wellness lecture. Its own site describes the drink as falling into its own category rather than masquerading as an alcohol-free spirit.

The result feels distinctive because it doesn’t need to apologise for what it isn’t.

What we rate:

They gave a growing category its own atmosphere instead of borrowing one from wine or spirits.

That’s a useful reminder for brands entering crowded spaces: you don’t always need to follow the category codes. Sometimes you need to build better ones.

The Ordinary: No beauty theatre required

The beauty industry has spent decades perfecting the art of making simple things sound like they were discovered in a secret laboratory beneath the moon.

The Ordinary took one look at that and said: absolutely not.

Its packaging looks clinical. Its products are named after their ingredients. Its pricing is direct. The whole brand feels like a quiet refusal to overcomplicate skincare for the sake of theatre.

That clarity became the point of difference.

The Ordinary made transparency feel confident. It understood that plenty of people were tired of vague promises, magical language and tiny jars that cost more than their weekly food shop.

What we rate:

They understood that clarity can be its own kind of confidence.

The Ordinary didn’t win by making skincare more aspirational. It won by making it easier to understand.

Patagonia: The outdoor brand that tells you to buy less

Most retail brands want you to keep buying.

Patagonia has built its identity around a more awkward idea: you probably don’t need more stuff.

That belief shows up in its repair services, Worn Wear programme, environmental campaigns, product guarantees and activism. Its website gives as much space to repair, circularity and action as it does to new products. It also states that it gives profits to the planet and supports grassroots activism.

That doesn’t mean Patagonia has somehow escaped capitalism. It is still a company selling jackets. But its mission changes how the business behaves, which makes the brand feel more credible than a purpose statement tucked away on an About page.

What we rate:

The mission isn’t parked on an About page. It shapes the business.

That’s the hard bit. Plenty of brands can write a nice statement about their values. Fewer are willing to make those values visible in their operations, products and decisions.

UPP: a VFX studio website that feels like entering the work

Universal Production Partners could have asked us to build them a standard studio website: showreels, service lists, a few impressive credits and a contact form.

Instead, we built a digital experience that feels closer to stepping through a portal into its world.

The site uses cinematic WebGL, changing time-of-day environments, game-inspired interactions and layered storytelling to reflect the scale, craft and imagination behind UPP’s work in film and television. It doesn’t simply tell visitors that the studio creates visual effects. It gives them a small taste of what that capability feels like.

Read the full case study here!

What we rate:

We got to make the website part of the brand experience, rather than a place to park the work. Your website shouldn’t only explain what you do. It can create the same feeling your best work creates, before someone has read a word.

The common thread

None of these brands rely on one clever logo, a trendy typeface or a campaign that happened to hit.

They’ve each made a clear choice about the world they want to build, then kept finding ways to express it.

For Ffern, that means ritual and seasonality. For Fishwife, it means turning the pantry into a playground. Vacation treats sunscreen like an entry ticket to a strange retro resort. Mother Root makes non-alcoholic drinking feel like its own occasion. The Ordinary makes clarity feel radical. Patagonia makes its values part of the product experience. UPP turns a VFX studio website into a cinematic experience.

That’s what committing to the bit looks like.

A strong brand idea should make decisions easier. It should tell you how your website feels, how your packaging behaves, what your campaigns sound like and which opportunities are worth saying no to.

The best brands don’t just look like themselves.

They act like themselves too.
